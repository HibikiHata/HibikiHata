What runs by itself keeps going. What depends on you stops.

Last updated 2026-11-12

Conclusion. In my own system, the parts that ran by themselves never stopped, and the parts that depended on me to fill them in all stopped, even with a reminder in place. A stopped habit is often a sign of a badly designed system, not of weak willpower. So instead of trying harder, I change the design. Three rules guide it: treat a gap as information, let the machine record and keep the decisions for yourself, and make starting again easy.

Habits stop where the system asks too much

Anything that runs on its own keeps running. Anything that waits for a person to act depends on that person's attention on every single day. That is the real difference between the two, and willpower alone does not close it.

The parts that stop usually ask more than they need to. There is more than one place to write, so every entry starts with a choice. The screen you check shows something other than what you actually do, so your work never appears where you look. What you record is never shown back to you, so recording has no visible use. Each of these is small, but each one has to be paid again every day.

A reminder does not fix a path that leads nowhere

The usual fix is a cue: a reminder or trigger that puts the habit in front of you. It helps when the only thing missing is remembering. In my case the journal already opened on its own, and it stayed empty. What was missing was not the reminder but a reason: a reason to write in that place, and a reason to read it later. If those are missing, a cue only puts an empty page in front of you more often.

Three rules for designing habits

  • Treat a gap as information, not a failure. When something stops, look at what the system asked of you before you look at yourself. Blame only makes starting again feel like admitting defeat.
  • Let the machine record, keep the decisions, and make the record come back. Bookkeeping that a program can do reliably, it should do. Keep the thinking and the deciding for yourself. And make sure whatever is recorded comes back to the place where you decide, or there is no reason to record it.
  • Make starting again easy. Gaps will happen. A system you can return to after time away is worth more than one that only works if you never miss a day. No streaks to break, and no backlog waiting when you come back.

What this does not show

"Shape the environment, not the willpower" is not a new idea, and this comes from one person's system, reviewed with the help of an AI assistant rather than tested. Willpower still matters. My point is narrower: when a habit stops, the design is the part you can actually change.
