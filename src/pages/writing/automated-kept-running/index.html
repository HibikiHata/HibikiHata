Everything I automated kept running. Everything I had to type in myself stopped.

Last updated 2026-11-12

Conclusion. In the first half of 2026, every part of my personal system that ran by itself kept going, and every part I had to fill in myself stopped within a few months, even though my notes app opened the journal for me every time it started. I don't think more willpower would have fixed that. For me, habits are something to design rather than something to force. I now build them on three rules: don't treat a gap as a failure, let the machine do the recording and make sure what is recorded comes back to me, and make it easy to start again.

The line ran between what needed me and what didn't

I keep my life in one personal system: tasks, notes, a daily journal, a habit log, and small programs that run on their own. When I looked through all of it in August 2026, the scheduled jobs and automatic logs had never stopped. The habit log, the journal, the task board and my notes had all stopped, one after another, between February and June.

Willpower may be part of the reason. But each part that stopped also asked more of me than it needed to. There were several places I could write, and I had to pick one each time. My task view was still showing a list I had abandoned, so the work I was actually doing never appeared there. Nothing I wrote was ever shown to me again, so writing had no visible use.

A reminder alone did not bring the journal back

The usual advice is to add a cue: something that puts the habit in front of you. I already had one, because the journal opened on its own. It stayed empty for months anyway. The journal was in front of me, but I still had no reason to write there rather than somewhere else, and no reason to read it later. In my case the cue was not the missing piece.

Three rules I now design by

  • A gap is information, not a failure. When something stops, I look at what the system asked of me, not at myself. Blaming myself only makes starting again feel like admitting defeat.
  • Let the machine record, keep the decisions, and make the record come back. Bookkeeping that a program can do reliably, it should do. What I keep for myself is the thinking and the deciding. And whatever gets recorded has to show up again where I make decisions, or there is no reason to keep it.
  • Make starting again easy. Gaps will happen. I would rather have a system I can return to after two weeks away than one that only works if I never miss a day. So no streaks to break, and no backlog waiting for me when I come back.

What this does not show

This is one person's system, and "build the environment, not the willpower" is not a new idea. I did not measure willpower, so I cannot rule it out. I looked back over the system with the help of an AI assistant, and the reading of the causes is ours, not a controlled test. What I can offer is one concrete case where a reminder was already in place and did not help, and the rules I took from it.
